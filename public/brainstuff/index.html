A field guide

How the brain works.

A compact walkthrough — from the spiking unit of computation all the way up to why you can still remember your phone number from middle school. Written for someone who knows what a gradient is and what a cell membrane is, but hasn't spent a semester on neuroscience.

01 The neuron

The cell that does the computing.

The brain has on the order of 86 billion neurons, plus a roughly equal number of support cells (glia). A neuron is a regular animal cell that has specialized into one job: receive electrical signals on one end, decide whether to fire, and send a signal out the other end.

The parts to remember are:

  • Dendrites — input branches. A typical cortical neuron has thousands of them, each receiving signals from other neurons.
  • Soma — the cell body. Adds up all the incoming signals and decides whether to fire.
  • Axon — the output cable. Just one per neuron, but it can branch a lot at the far end.
  • Myelin sheath — a fatty insulation wrapped around the axon at intervals. Lets the signal jump from gap to gap (a process called saltatory conduction) and makes it much faster.
  • Axon terminals — where the neuron's output meets the next neuron's input. The contact point is the synapse.
ML mapping

The cartoon version: dendrites are x_i, synapses are w_i, the soma does the weighted sum, and the axon carries the output. Where it breaks: real neurons don't output a single scalar — they output a spike train, dendrites do their own local nonlinear processing, and "weights" are physical machinery that can be on the order of dozens of milliseconds to update.

02 Spikes & action potentials

How a neuron actually "fires."

A neuron at rest sits at about −70 mV inside relative to outside. That gradient is maintained by an ion pump (the Na⁺/K⁺ pump) that constantly trades sodium ions out for potassium ions in. The cell is essentially a charged battery, kept charged by burning ATP.

When dendrites receive enough excitatory input, the voltage inside the soma rises. If it crosses a threshold around −55 mV, a chain reaction kicks off: voltage-gated sodium channels in the membrane fly open, sodium rushes in, the voltage spikes to about +30 mV, and then potassium channels open to bring it back down — overshooting briefly into a refractory period before resetting. That whole event is the action potential, or spike. It takes about 1–2 milliseconds.

Two consequences worth keeping in mind:

  • All-or-nothing. A spike's shape and amplitude don't depend on how strong the input was. The output is a binary event. Information is in the timing and frequency of spikes, not in their size.
  • Refractory period. For a millisecond or two after a spike, the neuron can't fire again. This caps the maximum firing rate at a few hundred Hz.
Quick sanity check

Action potentials propagate down the axon at roughly 1–100 m/s depending on diameter and myelination. Compare to electrons in a copper wire (~2/3 the speed of light). Brains are slow and parallel; CPUs are fast and serial.

03 The synapse

Where one neuron talks to the next.

When a spike reaches the axon terminal, it doesn't jump directly to the next neuron — there's a tiny gap (~20 nm) called the synaptic cleft. The signal converts from electrical to chemical to cross it.

The mechanism, in order:

  1. The spike arrives at the axon terminal, opening voltage-gated calcium channels.
  2. Ca²⁺ rushes in. Calcium triggers nearby vesicles (little membrane-bound bags pre-packed with neurotransmitter) to fuse with the cell membrane and dump their contents into the cleft.
  3. The released neurotransmitter (often glutamate or GABA) diffuses across, binds to receptors on the postsynaptic side.
  4. The receptor is an ion channel. Binding opens it, ions flow in, and the postsynaptic voltage shifts — either up (excitatory, EPSP) or down (inhibitory, IPSP).
  5. If the postsynaptic neuron's total input crosses threshold, it spikes, and the process repeats.

Excitatory (glutamate)

Pushes the receiving neuron toward firing. Roughly 80% of cortical synapses are excitatory.

Inhibitory (GABA)

Pushes it away from firing. Critical for keeping the network from runaway feedback — without inhibition, you get a seizure.

Neuromodulators ≠ neurotransmitters

Some chemicals (dopamine, serotonin, norepinephrine, acetylcholine) don't carry the main message — they tune how nearby synapses respond. Think of them as global signals injected into many circuits at once: "this moment was rewarding," "pay attention," "stay calm." They show up again in the learning section.

04 Plasticity & Hebbian learning

Why synapses are not static.

The connection strength between two neurons isn't fixed. It changes based on activity. This is synaptic plasticity, and it's the physical substrate of learning. The standard summary, attributed to Donald Hebb:

Hebb's rule

"Neurons that fire together, wire together." If neuron A consistently helps fire neuron B, the synapse from A to B gets stronger. If A consistently fails to help, it weakens.

The two flavors:

  • LTP (long-term potentiation) — repeated, well-timed activation strengthens the synapse. More receptors get inserted into the postsynaptic membrane, the same amount of neurotransmitter produces a bigger response.
  • LTD (long-term depression) — uncorrelated or badly-timed activity weakens the synapse. Receptors get pulled out.

Whether a synapse strengthens or weakens depends sensitively on timing: if the presynaptic spike arrives a few milliseconds before the postsynaptic neuron fires, LTP. If it arrives after, LTD. This is called spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP), and it's roughly the brain's local credit-assignment rule — a synapse gets credit when its input actually helped cause the output.

ML mapping

STDP is local — each synapse updates based only on what its own pre- and postsynaptic neurons did. There's no global error signal threading backward through the network like in backprop. This is one of the biggest open questions in computational neuroscience: how does the brain solve credit assignment without backprop? Candidates include feedback alignment, predictive coding, and dopamine-gated three-factor rules (the third factor being a neuromodulator that says now).

Plasticity also includes structural changes — entire new synapses forming, old ones pruned away, dendritic spines growing or shrinking. Most of this happens on the scale of hours to days, not milliseconds. Childhood and adolescence are periods of massive synaptic pruning: you start with roughly twice as many synapses as you'll end up with, and the brain throws away the ones that didn't get used.

05 Circuits, not neurons

A single neuron doesn't compute anything interesting.

Most of what the brain does happens at the circuit level — small groups of tens to thousands of neurons wired together. A few recurring motifs:

  • Feedforward: input → layer → layer → output. Standard "sensory processing" shape, e.g. retina → thalamus → V1 → V2 → … in vision.
  • Recurrent: neurons in a layer connect to each other. Lets a circuit hold a state over time — short-term memory traces, working memory.
  • Feedback / top-down: higher areas project back to lower ones, biasing what gets noticed. Attention and prediction live here.
  • Inhibitory interneurons: small local cells that gate or normalize activity. They're why the cortex doesn't seize.

A useful mental picture: every "thought," memory, or perception corresponds to a specific pattern of activity across many neurons — a vector in a very high-dimensional space. Concepts are not stored in single neurons (mostly). They're distributed.

06 How the brain learns

Three learning signals, layered on top of plasticity.

Plasticity gives the brain a mechanism for changing connection strengths. But you still need a signal that says which changes to make. The brain seems to use at least three, blended together:

Unsupervised / Hebbian

"What fires together wires together." Captures statistical structure in the input — natural images, sound, motion. No teacher needed; co-occurrence is the signal. This is most of what early sensory cortex learns.

Reinforcement (dopamine)

Midbrain dopamine neurons fire when an outcome is better than expected. The signal they broadcast is essentially a reward prediction error — TD-error in RL terms. Synapses that were recently active when dopamine fires get strengthened.

Supervised-ish

Pure supervised learning with labeled targets is rare in the brain. But error-correcting circuits (cerebellum, predictive coding in cortex) effectively learn from the gap between expected and actual sensory input — a form of self-supervised learning.

Neuromodulator gating

Acetylcholine and norepinephrine tell circuits "this moment matters — turn up plasticity." Plasticity isn't always on; it's strongly gated by attention and arousal. Boring inputs barely write to memory.

The dopamine = RPE story

Wolfram Schultz's monkey experiments in the '90s are the canonical result: train a monkey that a tone predicts juice, and the dopamine response gradually shifts from the juice onset to the tone onset. When the tone plays but no juice arrives, dopamine drops below baseline. This is a textbook TD(0) signal, computed by biology decades before anyone built it in code.

"Learning" in everyday usage spans wildly different timescales: perceptual learning (recognizing a new face) takes seconds to a few minutes; motor skill learning (a tennis backhand, a new piano piece) takes weeks of repetition and shows up as changes in motor cortex and cerebellum; declarative learning (facts you can state out loud) is fast at encoding but needs the hippocampus and consolidation, which is the next section.

07 Memory: kinds & timescales

There is no single "memory system."

Memory isn't one thing. It's several mechanisms operating on different timescales and stored in different physical places. The cleanest carving:

Working memory

Holding a phone number in your head while you dial it. Lasts seconds, capped at maybe 3–4 chunks. Implemented as persistent activity in recurrent loops in the prefrontal cortex — the neurons keep spiking to keep the pattern alive. Nothing is "stored" in the structural sense; it's a sustained activation. Stop attending and it's gone.

Declarative memory

Things you can state: episodes (what you ate yesterday) and facts (the capital of France). New declarative memories depend critically on the hippocampus, a curled structure in the medial temporal lobe. Patient H.M., who had both hippocampi removed for epilepsy in 1953, could not form a single new declarative memory afterward — but could still learn new motor skills, without remembering having practiced them.

Procedural memory

Skills and habits — riding a bike, typing, throwing a curveball. Stored in the basal ganglia and cerebellum, learned slowly through repetition, hard to verbalize. The system that handles this is mostly independent from the declarative one, which is why you can lose one without losing the other.

08 Consolidation & sleep

How a fast write buffer becomes a slow weight update.

The dominant theory of long-term memory formation is systems consolidation, and it maps very cleanly onto a thing ML people will recognize.

  • An experience happens. The hippocampus binds together the cortical patterns that were co-active — sight, sound, place, time — into a single sparse index. This is fast and one-shot. You don't have to see your friend's new haircut twice to remember it.
  • Over hours to years, the hippocampus replays these patterns, especially during sleep. Each replay reactivates the same cortical patterns. With repeated reactivation, the cortex slowly forms its own direct connections — the memory becomes independent of the hippocampus.
  • Eventually the cortical version is strong enough to stand alone. Damage to the hippocampus now will wipe out recent memories (still being consolidated) but spare old ones (already in cortex). This pattern — temporally graded retrograde amnesia — is exactly what's observed in hippocampal patients.
ML mapping

This is essentially experience replay, the same trick DQN uses. The hippocampus is a fast-write, high-capacity-but-finite buffer; the cortex is a slow-learning, distributed network that needs many shuffled passes over the same experiences to avoid catastrophic forgetting. Replay during sleep interleaves new memories with old ones — and the formal analogy is now tight enough that it's an active research area: complementary learning systems.

Sleep matters here for real, mechanistic reasons. During non-REM slow-wave sleep, the hippocampus produces bursts of fast activity called sharp-wave ripples, during which it replays sequences from the day, time-compressed maybe 10–20×. Disrupt these ripples experimentally in rats, and they fail to consolidate spatial memories from the previous day. Sleep is when the slow network gets trained.

09 The system view

A one-screen tour, then stop.

The brain isn't a uniform sheet of neurons. Major chunks specialize:

  • Cerebral cortex — the wrinkly outer sheet. Roughly six layers, ~2 mm thick. Most "high-level" computation: perception, language, planning, abstract thought. Divided into sensory areas (visual, auditory), motor areas, and association areas (prefrontal, parietal).
  • Hippocampus — episodic memory write buffer, also critical for spatial navigation (place cells, grid cells).
  • Basal ganglia — action selection and habit learning. Receives the dopamine reward signal.
  • Cerebellum — motor control, timing, error correction. Densely packed (it has more neurons than the rest of the brain combined, in tiny granule cells).
  • Thalamus — the relay hub. Almost all sensory information passes through it on the way to cortex.
  • Amygdala — emotional salience, especially fear. Tags memories as "important," which is why traumatic events are over-consolidated.
  • Brainstem & hypothalamus — the boring-but-keeping-you-alive layer: breathing, heart rate, temperature, sleep, hunger.

The two things to keep in mind about systems organization:

  1. Specialization is real but soft. The visual cortex really does do vision — but if you blind a person early enough, that same cortex repurposes for touch and hearing. Function is shaped heavily by inputs.
  2. Everything is recurrent. The connectivity diagram is not "thalamus → cortex → done." Cortex projects back to thalamus more strongly than the other way. Higher areas project back to lower ones. Loops everywhere. The brain is closer to a giant recurrent graph than a feedforward stack.
Where this leaves us

A 30-second summary you can carry around: neurons fire in spikes; synapses convert electrical to chemical and back; synaptic strengths change with use (plasticity); learning is plasticity gated by a few different signals (Hebbian, dopamine-RPE, neuromodulator-controlled attention); short-term memory is sustained activity, declarative long-term memory is consolidated from hippocampus to cortex via replay during sleep, and procedural memory lives in basal ganglia and cerebellum. The rest is — a lot — of detail on top.
